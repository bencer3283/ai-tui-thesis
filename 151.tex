\begin{abstract}
    Ever since OpenAI unveiled ChatGPT to the public, the idea of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) has stormed the tech industry with its promise to be ``the most impactful technology in human history''. (\cite{sama}) This is part of a larger paradigm shift of AI development towards general capability Foundation Models that consumes humongous resources. The conversational chatbot interface of ChatGPT has become the norm of how people expect to interact with AI. However, the paradigm of foundation model is dangerous. Enormous amounts of energy, labor and capital have been poured into the development of foundation model despite its several overlooked issues, like reinforced stereotypes and economic inequalities, unhealthy distortion of the science community and the environmental cost of datacenters. 

    To make things worse, the current interaction design paradigm in AI is heavily influenced by anthropomorphism, creating the false illusion of AI having human-level cognitive capabilities and fueling exaggerated excitement and fear around AI. The goal of this work is to disrupt this AI paradigm by proposing novel ways of interacting with Large Language Models. Specifically, employing the idea of designing machine-like interfaces that adhere closely to the nature of AI as a statistical pattern-matching algorithm for predicting natural language tokens. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In recent years, AI has definitely become the technology in the spotlight. AI startups are raising significant money every year, CEOs feel compelled to implement AI in their companies, while artists and news outlets are filing lawsuits against data infringement and the fear of being replaced by AI among workers is once again soaring. People seem to have mixed and strong feelings (perhaps too strong) about AI, but a proper conversation about what kind of AI we actually want is missing. The narrative around AI is usually extremely polarized -- either a utopian picture or dystopian one. On the utopian side, prominent voices in the industry describe AI as the ``the most impactful technology in human history'', (\cite{sama}) with the potential to solve cancer and climate change. On the other hand, an intelligence machine with super-human capability has been one of the greatest fears of humanity, dating way back from the original industrial revolution. (\cite{blood})

Neither of these narratives helps moving the technology forward to where we want it to be, because they include flooring either the throttle or the brake pedal. In reality, what AI actually can become deserves a much more granular answer, and through my research I wish to find out why it is so difficult for people to look at AI rationally. To begin with, I closely examined the internal properties and issues of the prevailing AI paradigm, investigating why, where and how it is worth improving. Subsequently, other alternative paradigms for AI development were explored, finally leading to my conclusion that anthropomorphism in the design of human-AI interface is the root of confusion and illusions.

The ultimate goal of this project is to provoke new ways of interacting with AI that are more grounded on the true affordances of the technology. I proposed the guiding principle of machine-like interface to confront the prevailing anthropomorphic paradigm. Through the designed tangible user interfaces, I wish people can start to see different ways AI can be built and used, in ways that meaningfully shape our lives.

\subsection{Issues of the current paradigm}
Almost all of the modern large language models (including multi-modal ones and image generation models) are based on an architecture called transformer. (\cite{bommasani2022opportunitiesrisksfoundationmodels}) Published by researchers in Google in 2017, (\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}) way before ChatGPT became popular around 2022, it is a type of machine learning model that specializes in understanding and generating natural language. It iteratively predicts the most likely next token (word) given an input paragraph.

The original transformer model does not possess the same level of conversational capabilities as GPT models. What OpenAI did to improve the capabilities of GPT was dramatically increasing the number of parameters inside the mathematical model. The original transformer model has up to 213 million parameters, while GPT-3 is almost 800 times bigger with its 175 billion parameter. (\cite{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners}) This paradigm of making models bigger in order to achieve better performance is famously characterized as OpenAI's Scaling Law. (\cite{scaling}) Behind this paradigm, its seemingly impressive capabilities are made possible by an extremely large amount of computing power and training data. The training of a state-of-the-art large language model requires tens of thousands of computing units running non-stop for weeks. Each computing unit can consume up to 300 watts of power when running at full speed. They also generate a lot of heat, which takes additional energy and water to dissipate. Power consumption of the largest modern datacenters is designed to consume around 150 megawatts, equivalent to nearly 122,000 American households.

These humongous models are also commonly referred to as Foundation Models, because they are large (in terms of number of parameters/weights of neural networks), possess general capability, and act as a foundation or intermediate for downstream AI products. (\cite{bommasani2022opportunitiesrisksfoundationmodels}) Their large scale means they are extremely expensive to develop, and only a handful of resourceful companies are able to afford the computing power, energy, water, and labor required. Their general capability means their behavior is unpredictable, and there is a pressing lack of proper benchmarks to evaluate their performance and externalities. They have a broad impact and implications as they are adapted by all kinds of AI applications; however, their intrinsic biases are also inherited by every downstream product, reinforcing and amplifying existing stereotypes and inequalities.

\subsection{Alternatives}
In the history of machine learning, the paradigm of foundation models is a relatively recent development. Over the years, prominent researchers have been advocating for various approaches towards building AI systems. \cite{10.5555/3042573.3042809} outlined a different paradigm which prioritizes real-world use cases instead of the pursuit of performance metrics. Other than trying to build the largest, most capable models, starting with specific applications in mind when building machine learning systems enables us to produce smaller, much more efficient models. This is a picture of various models with different architecture and ``knowledge'' for different tasks, as opposed to giant foundation models being adapted for every application. 

When models are smaller and requires less computing power to run and train, they can be hosted locally by the user and even be built with user's custom data. Their compartmentalized knowledge also means user's data stays within a confined region, making it more secure and private. Together with the fact that they can be customized by user's own data, it became a sharply contrasting paradigm -- models wholly owned and controlled by the user.

The same inspiration can also be drawn from \cite{reboot}'s theory about building AI systems that closer resemble the human mind. In particular, giving models means to memorize and understand human's internal representations can imply radically different human-AI relationship. The long-standing convention in machine learning is feeding training data to optimize the algorithm, leaving no interpretable mechanism that allows the end-user to program or configure the system. Humans, however, are able to internalize our experiences into our own unique logic like value, purpose, and desire.

If we build machine learning systems that allow these internal representations to be programmed in or taught to the AI, they will be able to adapt to each individual human better, potentially allowing us to trust AI system more. This is, of course, only desirable when the models are owned and controlled locally by the end user, not by private corporations. By scaling models bigger and bigger, we got a taste of radically new capabilities of AI, but many new issues also start to emerge. In my opinion, the future potential of AI lies in making these models smaller, and I wish to demonstrate what that future can look like.
\subsection{Tangible interface}
All of the issues and alternatives prove that AI deserves a much more informed conversation about how we want to use and develop it. Popular opinions on AI, however, often lie in the extreme polarized narrative of complete adoption or resistance. (\cite{resistance, adoption}) I think it is because people are fooled by the illusion of AI as a true human-level intelligence, and that illusion creates overly amplified fear and excitement. One prominent cause for this illusion, in my opinion, is anthropomorphism in the design of human-AI interfaces. The interaction design of AI has been trying to mimic human behavior, which obscures the true capacity and limitations of AI. This not only feeds people the wrong image but also creates several usability issues.

Ever since the popularity of ChatGPT skyrocketed, the designed interface for Large Language Models has been centered around various forms of chatbot. This makes sense, as these models are built to understand and predict natural language. However, chatbot alone is a very inefficient way to interact with these models. For example, in tasks that involve multiple iterative improvements, it becomes very difficult to track different ``versions'' of the task, and the conversation also gets tediously long. Additionally, chatbot as a human computer interface provides no clue to how the technology actually works, and thus deprives the user of learning best practices and trouble-shooting techniques. Furthermore, By simulating chatting -- a profoundly human behavior, the interface deceives its user about the true purpose of AI as a tool. Instead, this kind of human resemblance drives cognitive surrender and takes decision-making agency away from the user.

As an attempt to break through the illusions of AI, I designed several demonstrations of Tangible User Interfaces that are inspired by the idea of making them machine-like. These interfaces act as an experiment for new interaction paradigm for AI that is more frictional, attention-seeking and informative. By making the interactions closer resemble the true properties of AI, I wish to provoke new, informed conversations about where and how AI should be applied in the real world.

\section{Unseen downfalls}

\subsection{Natural resources}
As estimated by \cite{strubell-etal-2019-energy}, the training of a large language model can generate more than five times the carbon footprint of the lifetime of a car including fuel. Besides training, running these models is also an energy hungry task. According to \cite{10.1145/3630106.3658542}, the energy required to generate an image using large foundation models is equivalent to one fifth of the energy stored in an iPhone battery. Energy consumption of these datacenters is only predicted to continue rising, surpassing that of the entire state of Japan (1000 TWh) in 2026. (\cite{iea})

Humongous datacenters not only take electricity to run, they also require water for cooling down the computing units. \cite{10.1145/3724499} estimated that the training of OpenAI's 175 billion parameter model GPT-3 consumed 5.4 million liters of water in Microsoft's datacenters. Depending on where the model is hosted, it also consumes 500ml of water for every 10-50 medium-length responses generated.

\subsection{Labor}
As big techs race to spend tens of billions building out the biggest datacenters to support the foundation model boom, land, communities and nature environments are being exploited. On the outskirts of Santiago, Chile, the municipality of Quilicura hosts various factories and Google's datacenter. Only two decades ago, Quilicura was a rural town home to the rich biodiversity of the Atacama Desert. Now, there are only industrial complexes and the ``Quilicura Urban Forest'', built by Google as a community giveback in 2019. However, there are not many residents still living there as it has become an industrial area. Like most datacenters around the world, Google's datacenter also does not provide any job for the locals except for the temporary construction work -- in 2024, a job posting for mechanical technician for the datacenter requires applicants to submit resumes only in English. (\cite{empire})

Generative foundation models like GPT are trained on enormous amounts of datasets, many of which are impossible to filter or censor. As a result, the original models produce sensitive content, and companies like OpenAI hire Silicon Valley middlemen like Sama or Scale to access cheap labor in developing countries to moderate the generated content. It is reported that Sama hired people in Kenya for an average of \$1.46 to \$3.74 to do this work. They are exposed to sexual, violent text content including child sexual abuse, slavery, and bestiality while their employer provides minimum mental health support, which often results in serious mental health conditions. (\cite{empire})

These examples highlight the multifaceted impact of western technological advancements. They exploit resources from the underprivileged and destroy their land, community, and lifestyle.

\subsection{Bias and stereotypes}
Foundation models act as a general intermediate for various AI products; they are often fine-tuned, augmented, or adapted for more specific use cases. Because they are trained with extremely large datasets like the Common Crawl, the bias of the datasets is inherited by the models. For example, there is more content on the internet in English than in any other language. The large amount of online content that is hateful, abusive, and toxic inevitably becomes part of the training material and also directs the model's behavior. The overrepresentation, underrepresentation, and misrepresentation across cultural, geographical, and racial groups are also carried through to foundation models.

These intrinsic biases are inherited by downstream applications that adapt foundation models, creating widespread performance disparities for various use cases. For example, \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.1915768117} looked into how African American English speakers who cannot access reliable speech recognition technologies are in a disadvantageous position when these technologies are used in job interviews or transcribing courtroom proceedings. According to \cite{Hofmann2024}, models exhibit stereotypes about speakers of African American English. They may be more likely to suggest that speakers of African American English be assigned less-prestigious jobs, be convicted of crimes, and be sentenced to death. \cite{10.1145/3442188.3445928} found that models fail to detect faces of people with darker skin tones.

\subsection{Data ripoff}
On top of that, the training data are often obtained without clear consent. Several news outlets have filed lawsuits against foundation model developers like OpenAI and/or Anthropic for illegally using their publications as training materials. These materials are usually consumed by the model training to update its parameters. They only exist in the generic form of numbers and vectors, erasing the identity and intention of the original authors. Image generation models are trained on images created by artists and illustrators, only to eventually replace them in the workplace.

Actress Scarlett Johansson famously accused OpenAI of creating a ChatGPT voice assistant that sounded almost identical to her even after she declined to license her voice to OpenAI. ``I was shocked, angered and in disbelief that Mr. Altman would pursue a voice that sounded so eerily similar to mine that my closest friends and news outlets could not tell the difference,'' she said. (\cite{scarlett}) In response, OpenAI's CEO Sam Altman stated that the model was trained on the voice of another actor.

\subsection{Economic inequality}\label{econ}
It's easy to describe AI as a groundbreaking technological advancement that benefits all of humanity, solving climate change, cancer, or other troubling issues that pose a significant danger to our species. However, none of these promises seem remotely close to being realized, and the actual economic impact of AI paints a much grimmer picture.

\cite{NBERw32487} estimates the Total Factor Productivity\footnote{In simpler terms, the combined productivity of production inputs (capital and labor). Traditionally its growth is attributed to technological advancement.} growth generated by AI to be 0.66\% over a ten year period, which is much more modest than people might expect. Moreover, as estimated by \cite{690378712}, emerging technologies like social media may have negative impact on social welfare even if they create economic growth on paper. We've already seen various malicious and manipulative uses enabled or exacerbated by AI, like disinformation. (\cite{csetbuchanan})

According to \cite{10.1093/cjres/rsz022}, when new technologies are able to drive substantial productivity growth, even if a large portion of routine tasks are automated and thus lower-skill workers are replaced, the reorganization of production processes creates new tasks and jobs for human labor. However, if the productivity growth is modest or if the new technology's sole focus is to replace human labor, new tasks may not emerge, and task displacement means low-skilled workers will suffer from job loss. This is what Acemoglu described as ``so-so'' automation, and AI, with its estimated 0.66\% productivity growth, seems dangerously close to falling into this group.

Recent developments in economics suggest that technology, in particular those that enable automation in the production process of goods or services, creates an imbalance and unequal outcome for people in different income or demographic groups. (\cite{https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA19815, https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA19417}) Automation usually replaces low-skilled labor in the production process. In other words, the people who lose their jobs to AI are theoretically those who are already in the lower income bracket. Additionally, more automation means a higher return on capital investment, delivering more earnings to the wealthy. \cite{SKARE2024102719} shows that the capital stock of AI is unevenly distributed and positively correlated with exacerbated income inequality. Using online job posting data, \cite{ssrn4874061} finds that hiring for lower-level office jobs declined after ChatGPT's release, while demand for higher-skilled workers increased.

Economic inequality not only exists across income groups but also across demographics. Preliminary estimation in \cite{NBERw32487} suggests that AI might cause the wages of low-education women to decline. \cite{appelmccrorytamkin2025geoapi} finds that countries with higher AI usage are concentrated in North America, Western Europe, and Northeast Asia, while the global south almost entirely falls into the minimum usage group. In their theoretical framework, \cite{doi:10.1086/737233} found that AI is more likely to displace workers from complex problem-solving work to routine tasks in a developing economy, unlike in an advanced economy where people will be better positioned to use AI to help them tackle more complicated work. All of this seems to suggest that the prevalence of AI is empowering the already-empowered while underprivileged groups will benefit less from AI.

Rising economic inequality has significant implications. Long-lasting stagnant wages and loss of employment opportunities in certain groups create popular discontent that sometimes fuels disruptive populist movements. Uneven distribution of income also leads to the consolidation of wealth for certain individuals or business sectors, which in turn results in the consolidation of power. This is particularly true in the case of the current AI landscape, in which very few people possess immense power over the trajectory of its development and are able to steer the future of AI to benefit themselves.

\subsubsection{Death of Open Science}
The paradigm of foundation models represents the homogenization in the field of machine learning research and development. Specifically, \cite{bommasani2022opportunitiesrisksfoundationmodels} characterize homogenization as ``the consolidation of methodologies for building machine learning systems across a wide range of applications.'' The origin of foundation models stems from the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), with key breakthroughs including self-supervised learning, word embedding, the transformer (\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}), and the bi-directional encoder-decoder (\cite{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional}). These technical architectures represent the essential properties of foundation models, laying out their uncanny capabilities but also their intrinsic downfalls.

Today, almost all state-of-the-art NLP models are derived from a handful of foundation models like the bidirectional encoder model BERT. (\cite{bommasani2022opportunitiesrisksfoundationmodels}) The transformer architecture of large language models is also beginning to be widely applied to other areas like speech recognition, image, biology, and reinforcement learning. The phenomenon of foundation models as a paradigm taking over other forms of machine learning systems is the key motivation of this work, particularly because of the ongoing issues it's creating and the huge amount of resources required to develop them. AI, as a frontier discipline of science, should be explored openly. However, we are observing foundation models crowding out the resources for research and development in other machine learning systems. 

Even worse, the development of foundation models is so expensive that only a handful of ``big-techs'' can afford it now. It's getting more and more difficult for independent researchers unaffiliated with big-tech companies to conduct research in cutting-edge foundation models. Because of the sheer size of these models, their performance claimed by the private developers is extremely hard to verify independently. Evaluating their real-world cost and externalities has become equally difficult for the same reason.

Founded as a non-profit to ensure the safety of AI development, OpenAI stopped open-sourcing its models after GPT-3 in 2020, citing security reasons. This has become the norm across many big players in the field, including Google and Anthropic. (\cite{empire}) Subsequently, OpenAI has transformed itself into a private for-profit company. The field of cutting-edge foundation model development increasingly looks like another technology race in Silicon Valley, rather than an open community in which members share knowledge and breakthroughs with each other for the advancement of science that benefits humanity. If AI is what we believe to be the next leap forward in human civilization, the path we are taking to get there seems like an extremely dangerous one.

The thriving community of AI Ethics researchers set out to tackle these threats by collaborating on research evaluating the issues of foundation models and coming up with appropriate benchmarks. Nonetheless, big-techs still find their way to try to consolidate their power in this community. \cite{10.1145/3461702.3462563} finds that big-techs use the same strategy as big tobacco in the 1950s to undermine research questioning their products. They publicly claim their emphasis on and care for AI safety and even set up internal research divisions to conduct research. They also sponsor and provide funding for top conferences, events, and individuals in AI Ethics. This presents conflicts of interest and threats to academic integrity. According to the cited work, there are more researchers in AI Ethics at top institutions that have received funding from or been affiliated with big-techs than those who have not. In 2020, researcher Timnit Gebru was ousted by Google over the publication of her now-famous paper Stochastic Parrots, highlighting the issues of large language models merely repeating their own training data. (\cite{empire}, \cite{parrots})

\section{Forgotten paths}
Known as the ``father of AI'', cognitive and computer scientist Marvin Minsky wrote in his book \textit{The Society of Mind}, ``The power of intelligence stems from our vast diversity, not from any single, perfect principle.'' He portrays the human mind as a society of simple but very different cognitive processes, also known as agents. (\cite{societyofmind}) Each of these individual thinking entity has different internal logics, combined to form the vast intelligence of our human mind.

The current paradigm in Artificial Intelligence, however, is devoted to building systems that are the complete opposite. The paradigm of foundation model suggests that a single, humongous ``master algorithm'' will be able to reproduce the extremely complicated and inter-weaved cognitive capabilities of the human mind. In particular, the uprise of Large Language Models implies that language is the only necessary tool for intelligent systems to reason and make decisions. Instead of incorporating a fundamentally different architecture for vision capabilities, the recent development of multi-modal Vision-Language Models uses the same word embedding techniques for understanding images, essentially treating each small ``patch'' of an image as a word. (\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words}) It seems like the world firmly believes that this one single way of developing AI systems can lead to something on par with the human mind.

The core proposition of this thesis is that instead of pursuing an omni-capable master algorithm, resources should be more equally devoted to developing various kinds of AI systems. This section outlines the other alternative paradigms of AI that I believe should have been given the same amount of attention. Some of them draw inspiration from the intrinsic downfalls of foundation models, others from the negative externalities we are observing around the world, and still others from the cognitive science that depicts how human intelligence works.

\subsection{Application-Driven AI}
The premise of foundation models, or more specifically transformer-based large language models, as the major breakthrough towards artificial general intelligence is that one giant model will be sufficient to encapsulate all sorts of human intelligence. Indeed, in recent years, we've seen substantial improvement in the performance of these models. However, The so-called ``performance'' are usually evaluated with hypothetical datasets and metrics that doesn't translate to real-world capabilities.

This is what Kiri Wagstaff of the California Institute of Technology describes as ``machine learning for machine learning's sake''. (\cite{10.5555/3042573.3042809}) A lot of these improvements don't necessarily translate to real-world usages. For example, a computer vision model might be able to successfully detect pedestrians with a 99.9\% rate on a benchmark dataset; nevertheless, failing to identify one pedestrian in every 1000 is still fatal and doesn't mean the model is nearly close to being capable enough to be applied to critical applications like autonomous vehicles. The long tradition of machine learning is based on a stochastic process instead of deterministic decision-making. Much of artificial intelligence's vast improvement in performance is driven by raising the probability of success, not real-world usage. Moreover, the same metric for success rate is not comparable across different applications. 99.9\% might not be enough for self-driving cars but might be sufficient for identifying food ingredients. This is again to say that the pursuit of performance in benchmarking metrics tells us nothing about how usable it is in real-world applications.

Additionally, Wagstaff also argues that the machine learning community needs to engage with real-world domain experts more closely. The prevailing paradigm of collaboration with other domain experts usually only happens for curating and annotating training datasets (\cite{10.1145/3411764.3445518}), but little for creating the appropriate evaluations for real-world applications and for their potential impact.

Creating models that have real-world applications in mind from the initiation of their development also boasts the benefit of having smaller, more efficient models. Over the past couple of years, we've seen developments in machine vision, audio, and robotics all converge to the paradigm of a multi-modal transformer architecture. The one-model-to-rule-them-all concept does seem tempting, but the truth is that different tasks may require different architectures for more efficient computing. Even having more application-specific transformers may also be beneficial.

Smaller models also enable the possibility of being run locally on smaller hardware, which allows their users to have a very different relationship with the models. Models that can be run locally will also be easier to be trained and fine-tuned by their users, which means they can adapt more granularly to a user's personal needs and lifestyle.

\subsection{Cognitive Algorithm}\label{cognition}
If the goal of Artificial Intelligence is to build computing systems that rival human intelligence, it makes sense to mimic how the human mind works. Unfortunately, the human brain and mind are such complicated systems that to this date, we still haven't solved their mystery. However, psychology, cognitive and neural science are still able to give us some hints.

New York University professors Gary Marcus and Ernest Davis wrote in their book \textit{Rebooting AI} that drawing from Kant's \textit{Critique of Pure Reason}, ``time, space, and causality are the three philosophical grounds fundamental to the human mind.'' (\cite{reboot}) When seeing a car coming while trying to cross the road, humans are able, in almost no time, to estimate how far the car is, how long it's going to take for it to be dangerously near, and how long it would take to cross the road, and then make a decision. Such simple everyday tasks are usually comprised of many complicated thoughts. We are able to ``sense'' the distance and time of the car with little to no brain effort, at the same time considering other factors, like the sound volume of the coming car or whether you are about to be late or not.

\subsubsection{Purely Analog Senses}
Given a well-defined context and sufficient knowledge, existing machine learning systems might be able to understand the time duration of a certain event, but they are only able to do so by inducing from mathematical formulas. They have the knowledge of time, but humans have a sense of time. Similarly, AI has an understanding of the shapes of objects, but it might not be able to immediately induce their functionality from the shapes. Imagine seeing a cheese grater for the first time; we are able to reason with its scale, shape, material, and texture to immediately guess what it might be for and how to use it. This kind of holistic reasoning combining our innate understanding of time, space, and causality is exactly the kind that intelligent machines should learn from the human mind. Of course, by showing a cheese grater and its text description to a model during training, current machine learning systems would have no problem understanding what a cheese grater is. But perhaps that's why foundation models have to be large models consuming almost all the available existing data to train. It is important to note that fundamentally, current state-of-the-art models are still language models that understand everything through language and language only. Computers fundamentally cannot ``sense'' anything but can understand its ``abstraction'' as language or other symbolic systems, like a mathematical formula.

A maybe more efficient way to build AI systems is to look into the other ``senses'' that do not yet exist in the digital world so that AI can reason and induce more holistically. Upon seeing the picture of a textile, we are immediately able to guess its texture, flexibility, or even whether it's waterproof or not, all without touching it. The existing ``senses'' computers have include light, sound, force, temperature, electrical potential, magnetic fields, and more. All of these are mathematically well-defined physical properties, and they all have corresponding sensors that can transform them into digital readouts. We humans, however, do not rely on the understanding of physical properties to perceive the world. We understand the world through smell, texture, haptics, and more. Machine learning systems, on the other hand, do not possess this kind of capability to understand these ``mathematically ill-defined'' and thus purely analog properties. 

Some recent developments in the field of the so-called world model claim to be able to generate realistic simulations of the physical world. (\cite{bruce2024geniegenerativeinteractiveenvironments,gupta2022maskvitmaskedvisualpretraining}) What these models actually are is models capable of playing video games. It is uncertain whether they are just reproducing common physical phenomena in video training data or if they indeed have an understanding of the rules of physics. Even if they do, their output is still limited to just video or visual images -- far from all the complicated analog senses humans have. We should tap into these unknown territories of the digital world, trying to come up with more abstraction or symbolic systems to teach machines more nuances of this physical world. This can potentially eliminate the vast resources required to teach models everything with empirical training materials. And in the context of the current paradigm of large language foundation models, giving computing devices more ``senses'' to characterize the world also saves us the effort and data size of trying to describe everything in text.  

\subsubsection{Common Senses and Causality}
When we say Novak Djokovic has been playing professional tennis for 20 years, we don't mean that he has been playing tennis non-stop for the continuous time duration of 20 years. The human mind is capable of understanding the implied meaning that tennis has been Novak Djokovic's profession for 20 years. However, a language model that is trained to understand the literal meaning of texts might not be able to decipher the latent implication.\footnote{I prompted Claude Sonnet 4.5, ``suppose one year is 365 days, one day is 24 hours, one hour is 60 minutes. Novak Djokovic has been playing tennis for 20 years, how long has he been playing tennis?'', and then the chatbot went on to actually convert 20 years to hours and minutes. Although it is also quite astonishing that the model was able to understand the implied meaning of the first part of the prompt, a human being would probably first ask for clarification.} This is an example of what we vaguely describe as ``common sense''. We, as humans, do not need to be taught that it is impossible to continuously play tennis for 20 years, even for a professional athlete, and are able to comprehend the idea of a profession because we are able to understand the world and human lives by employing different knowledge frameworks simultaneously. Instead of reading 20 years as 365 days and one day as 24 hours, our sense of 20 years as ``a very long time'' substantially outweighs the symbolic meaning of a year as a unit of time. The human mind is firstly a sensing entity and then an abstraction decoder. We built the various symbolic systems based on our innate senses; machine learning systems, however, can only induce from abstractions and symbols. As a result, the lack of common sense has been a challenge for AI.

An alternative AI paradigm is trying to program common sense, or the general understanding of causality, into purely mathematical models. We understand the basic causal relationship of everyday events and objects, but even the largest foundation model today does not have any mechanism to employ this kind of knowledge. It only has the most surface-level understanding of our common sense even after being trained on the largest corpus of text material. Carnegie Mellon University computer scientist Douglas Lenat was an advocate for intelligent machine systems with programmed causality. (\cite{LENAT1984269}) His long-term project Cyc aims to create a symbolic system and a knowledge base database that maps out the relationship of everyday common sense and implicit knowledge. It's a decision-making algorithm that is based on purely deterministic and pre-programmed relationships. After almost 40 years of employing philosophers and programmers to write down rules of the world for machines to understand, the project unfortunately never gained much traction. However, it is a sharp contrast to the strictly stochastic and training-based paradigm of foundation models. 

Amidst the enormous resources being poured into developing large models, maybe it is a good time to revisit the idea that machines should be able to employ some simple pre-programmed rules that describe how the world works, instead of trying to make the algorithms learn every single relationship embedded in our common sense. 

\subsubsection{Learning versus Innateness}
Large foundation models, as statistical pattern-matching machines, try to generalize everything into a widely applicable algorithm. Even if they were able to learn the entire common sense of the human mind from a large corpus of data, not everything follows rules. Our human intelligence makes decisions based on not only the abstraction and generalization of our various senses and experiences but also our innate values. We internalize our experiences into an individual's unique logic.

Psychology provides us with plenty of evidence that human decision-making does not only come from learnings from the outside world but also from internalized values. Psychologist John Watson of Johns Hopkins University, a major pioneer in \textit{behaviorism} psychology in the early 1900s, famously claimed that a child's behavioral pattern can be dictated purely by controlling every factor of the environment in which the child was raised. (\cite{behaviorism}) Only a couple of decades later, Noam Chomsky, who is usually seen as the founder of \textit{cognitive} psychology, published the groundbreaking work in linguistics \textit{Syntactic Structures}. (\cite{noamchomskylinguistic}). Along with his other works, he characterizes language understanding not as pattern-matching of past encounters of sentences but as a result of internalized grammar. (\cite{597748ff-2020-3c20-9c06-e358280e09a2}) The old perspective of behaviorism explains behavioral patterns entirely with an external reward system; in contrast, Noam Chomsky's cognitive psychology attributes the driving force of human intelligence to internal representations like desires, beliefs, and purpose. To this date, this view from cognitive psychology is still agreed upon by scholars, while behaviorism theories have vanished. (\cite{reboot})

The paradigm of foundation models, in a sense, is like dictating a child's behavior by raising them in a controlled environment. Every model starts from a bunch of random numbers and then learns everything from data by maximizing rewards or minimizing loss. Although so far we've seen this approach make big progress, it is an extremely resource-intensive process, and we must remember that this is not how the human mind works. We should look into ways to design models that are capable of internalizing values and generating internal representations of these values. Structuring experiences and learnings to create a unique internal logic is a crucial ``human'' aspect of human intelligence. Artificial intelligence should also be able to replicate its user's internal representation in the training/inferencing process. Most large language models might be able to adapt to a user's response, but they do so by appending the user's previous feedback to future prompts. Internally, it's just the same model processing longer inputs with more added context. Compared to the human mind's capability of generating internal representations, this is extremely inefficient and again adds to the already enormous resources required to run these models.

\section{Observations}
\subsection{AI alone is not good enough}
By saying that AI would benefit the world because it can solve cancer, climate change and poverty, popular AI advocative sentiment usually implies that if we solve AI, then we essentially solve everything. However, the actual implication of a solved world is far beyond general prosperity and panacea. Philosopher Nick Bostrom describes the condition of a solved world as \textit{Technology Maturity}, meaning that with AI we would have all the technologies we can possibly have and use them to solve whatever problems that remain. (\cite{bostromdeeputopia}) When we look into what exactly these technologies are, it became clear that the solved utopia is still very far away from us even with the advancement in AI. For example, all kinds of manufacturing should be efficient and automated, this will require distributed robotics systems at all physical scale, from heavy-industries like ship-building to molecular-scale actuators for things like pharmaceuticals. In today's world, even car manufacturing is still far from being fully automated.

At technology maturity, we can achieve anything we want with little to no effort, because every goods or services can be produced efficiently with fully automated processes. Even activities like schooling or reading would become obsolete because the production and transmission of knowledge can be seamlessly automated. This is a prime example of the need of a very sophisticated brain-computer interface. This interface would be able to extract knowledge from a human brain, digitize the so-called "knowledge" into an universal format, and then insert the knowledge into another brain. This would require at least two things that are still nowhere close to being feasible. First, we need to understand how the human brain stores and utilize memories. Second, micro-scale machines needs to be build to interact with the millions of neurons in our brain. This is a extremely speculative scenario, but it is one that fits into the utopian narrative. My point here is not to argue that AI can not contribute to building a solved world but to point out that AI is only a small part of it. AI is certainly an substantial technological advancement, but more has to come for us to inch towards technology maturity. The AI we have now can only deal with languages and computer files, but the vast majority of the world is still yet digitized.

All of these discussions about utopia, however, is only meaningful if we believe an utopian society can be achieve by technological advancement, and if we actually want to live in such an utopian world.

\subsection{Do we really want AI to be that good?} \label{moralstatus}
We are sold the utopian picture of AI enabling technology maturity by being a superintelligence entity that can solve other problems and advance civilization. However, the holistic implication of such a technology is seldom discussed. The AI we have created now is a statistical pattern-matching algorithm that largely relies on the understanding of language. We already established that this alone is not enough towards technology maturity. The question is, at which point does the ``intelligence'' of such a technology grow beyond a point where we can no longer just call it an algorithm? The level of intelligence required to solve some of the challenges of technology maturity is high that AI at that level might start to have consciousness, then creating and dictating such a technology would involve a whole host moral and ethical implications.

The idea of moral status becomes significant in this discussion. \cite{bostromdeeputopia} characterizes moral status as ``the idea that certain beings have properties that make them deserving being treated with moral consideration for their own sake: their well-being, interests, preferences, or right ought to be taken into account in our decision-making and judgement, and they ought to be treated not just as means but as ends in themselves.'' In attempting to create a machine with human-level intelligence, at some point it becomes inevitable when the machine is ``close enough'' to a human that we can no longer treat it just as a machine anymore. When is it ``close enough?'' Besides the obvious one of having consciousness, philosophers has developed several potential ``threshold'' of inducing moral status, such as ``having sophisticated conception of oneself as persisting through time; having agency and the ability to pursue long-term plans; being able to communicate and respond to normative reasons; having preferences and powers; standing in certain social relationships with other beings that have moral status; being able to make commitments and to enter reciprocal arrangements.'' You could even argue that some of them are already met.

If inducing moral status on these ``sentient robots'' is inevitable, what is the moral implication of creating them and controlling them with the sole purpose of serving us? Bostroom thinks that there might be fundamental difference between employing these robots and slavery, but is the picture of humans being served by robots that has feelings really a preferable picture of humanity?

Another, perhaps more radical philosophical speculation of technology maturity is the return of the Malthusian economy. If we take the economical analysis in Sec. \ref{econ}, where all the income from production goes to capital accumulation. AI robots will take over all of the labor and even the creation of new intellilectual properties. At that point, in the most extreme case, land on the earth will become the only scarce resource (because even the tiniest robot still takes up space), which means landowners will become the only people who are able to have a higher-than-subsistence level of income. The rest of the people will earn income that is just enough to keep them ``not hungry.''

Why? Because at technology maturity, we've ``maxxed out'' all the potential means of productivity growth. In a solved world, there is nothing to be improved. Every production process will be operating at optimal efficiency, and there is no economic growth to support the growth of population. \cite{bostromdeeputopia} points out that this condition is akin to the human history before the Industrial Revolution, when almost all of the economic activity is based on agriculture production. Becuase there was no productivity growth, the number of potatoes produced by per unit land is fixed. The only way to increase production is by growing potatoes on more land. For the later part of human history, we are able to design, improve and use tools, machines or technology to increase the amount of production per unit input, and that's the reason the economy can grow at a faster speed than population. At technology maturity, the tools, machines and technology are already at its optimal state, and there is no source of growth. The only difference is that instead of owning shovels to grow potatoes, we own robots to produce whatever the most efficient and advanced food that people will invent.

This picture of extreme inequality certainly does not sound appealing. Even though in such a world the just-above-subsistence level of living standard might still be much higher than today, the grim and purposeless life doesn't really feel worthwhile. It is also a world where people don't really work anymore, you own the ultimate production tool -- highly advanced robots -- that will do all the work for you. And everyone does that. As we are being sold the splendid image of a world with no poverty, hunger and disease, maybe we should carefully think about whether life in that world will be more and less colorful than the life we have now.

\subsection{AI is machine, not human}
The discussion of the long-term utopian (or dystopian) vision and speculation of AI is beyond the scope of this design work. We are designing for the world with current technologies and socioeconomical conditions. However, extrapolating the extreme future informs us a lot about what the current version of AI we have actually is and how it sits in the imaginable roadmap of the technology. Only once we have those understandings can we design for the AI we have instead the AI we envision.

Following previous discussions, we know that the current version of AI we have is still far away from what's promised. That is, the technology itself is still not good enough. However, in the discipline of design, we are designing the derivative product or human interface of AI \textit{as if} the technology was that good. The perimeter of the very limited capability of models is intentionally invisible. The intrinsic downfalls and externalities are hidden. In pursuing ease-of-use and ``seamless user experience'', user-configurablity is completely abandoned. It seems that designers want users to believe that the models can figure out what exactly what's needed when in fact more means of user input could potentially improve the performance dramatically. The prevalent chatbot interface gives up on informing the user how models work, essentially depriving user the opportunity to make models work better for them.

Chatbot is a human-human interface that lives on computing devices, not a human-computer interface. One of the implications of technology maturity is that whatever intelligence machine we come up with, we can interact with it in a manner extremely similar to human-human interaction. AI, as it is now, is a sort of intelligence machine that is still far from technology maturity. However, that interface that we came up with is the one that is supposed to make sense only once technology maturity is reached. In designing a interface like chatbot, the desginer forgot the fact that AI is still fundamentally a machine. Machines need to be operated in a certain way to yield maximum performance and efficiency. Operating a machine requires that the user has some technical knowledge. A well-planned learning curve is what makes a human interface usable. Typing on a keyboard is a learning curve. Scrolling, pinch-to-zoom, long-press on a touch screen all demand learning. For a technology like AI that is still novel to the public, opting for a human-like interface that has close to no learning curve seems more like evading the hard work rather than being considerate.

In every other technology humanity has ever seen before, its human interface is always one that bridges the needs of users and the limitations of the technology. The purpose of human interface is to make the technology work for the human, hence the interface has to work for both the technology and the human. Chatbot, however, is one that only works for human but takes no consideration for the intrinsic properties and operational parameters of the models.

A good human interface informs the user the capabilities (and limitations) of the technology, best operational practices and potential means of trouble-shooting. Every usable machine in the history has a human interface that accomplishes these things. When a machinist is operating a lathe, they can observe whether the power, gear ratio and cutting tool are making a clean cut. If they want to change the speed of the rotor, it's clear that the most preferable way is to change the gear ratio. If the cut is not clean even under optimal rotational speed, they would know maybe the cutting tool needs to be sharpened. Using a lathe requires a somewhat steep learning curve, but at least there is one. On a desktop computer interface, the relative size of the menu bar vaguely indicates the capacity for multitasking. You can easily drag and resize each window to have the best combination of applications running. If the computer becomes slow and laggy, most people can easily induce that they should close some windows to keep everything running smoothly. These are all examples of how a human interface bridges the gap between what the user wants to do and what the technology can do. 

What the chatbot interface assumes is that the user can do all of these by giving more instructions in natural language to correct or optimize the model's performance. If the AI technology we have is good enough in terms of its cognitive capabalities (see Section \ref{cognition}), this is a quite viable assumption. However, our AI is still not there and this chatbot interface only results in prompts augmented with more and longer prompts, which ultimately exceeds the context window of the model and makes it disoriented. Chatbot is an interface for the AI that can do everything (or, one that is very close to reach technology maturity), but it is an extremely ineffective and inefficient way to ``operate'' today's large language models.

Large language models, however, are inherently built to be interacted with natural language. Language is what they are trained to understand and predict. Discarding natural language altogether seems overly aggresive. On the other hand, the successful invention and use of language did indeed contributed substantially to the progression of human civilization and intelligence. If we are trying to teach machines our human intelligence, starting with languge doesn't seems like a bad idea at all.

In my mind, the better way to interact with LLMs would be natural language augmented with granular control over how the model actually works. In the history of machine and computer, the human interface always cleverly communicates how it works so that the user can use it in the most optimal way. Chatbot is a sensible clue to how LLM works, but there should be more.

\section{Against anthropomorphism}
Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) is a specific architecture of LLM that powers most of the AI chatbots today. When OpenAI unveiled it to the public as a product, they made two important decisions that dramatically altered the public perception of AI:
\begin{itemize}
    \item they decide to call it ChatGPT, and
    \item the user interface is a conversational chatbot.
\end{itemize}
In my opinion, this indicates that they leaned all the way into anthropomorphism, trying to make interacting with the technology feel as humanly as possible. They probably just wanted to build an application that best showcases the breakthrough performance of the LLM and creates some buzz, but in doing so, the entire conversation around AI became so confused about what AI actually is.

An LLM is fundamentally a machine learning algorithm designed to understand and predict natural language tokens, but language is different from conversation and chatting. Language is a specific type of abstraction and coding system that we use to communicate. From an information theory perspective, a conversation is so much more than just language. Conversation is inherently human because it encapsulates communications through language, tone, pace, facial expressions, and body language. The modes of information transmitted through a conversation are so much more than language alone, and are often ones that we associate with human interaction exclusively. This is why people decided that emoji (and the early days of : ) and : ( and ><) should be an indispensable part of texting.

By wrapping the language prediction capability of an LLM with a conversational interface, the perception of AI starts to shift from a predictive algorithm to a human-like chatbot. From a marketing perspective, good job. But in reality, what they actually did was create the illusion of something that has human-level intelligence, knows everything about the world, (potentially) can have access to all your data and memory, is always friendly and supportive or even wise, and you can just chat with. That's basically a god.

Except it isn't, and that confuses everyone. That illusion overly amplified both the excitement and fear around AI. Suddenly everyone is talking about things like p(doom) or AI solving cancer and climate change. People are distracted from the ground truth of AI and a bunch of its problems that are worth solving, things like resource consumption, power concentration, exacerbated inequality, and stereotypes. These are arguably the more pressing conversations we should be having about AI, but unfortunately, it's too easy to be fooled by science-fiction illusions.

\subsection{Why anthropomorphism is bad for AI}
To study how we can make a better interface for AI (and compete with god), let's start by looking at the reasons why we shouldn't converse with a computer.
\subsubsection{Affordance}
\cite{boo-chatbot} wrote that ``text inputs have no affordances.'' As a human-computer interface, a chatbot informs the user nothing about the technology. The only thing you can learn from it is that ``I can talk to this computer.'' Other than that, it doesn't tell you what the best way to use it is, what its capabilities and limitations are, and how to troubleshoot if something isn't going in the way you wanted. A chatbot as an interface has no structure and hierarchy, which makes it difficult for the user to assess and understand the technology. A good human-computer interface is the negotiated deal that satisfies a human's expectations and the technology's limitations. In the case of a chatbot, it's a total surrender to the wild science-fiction imagination of talking to a machine.

At the surface-level, it makes the technology difficult to use. We've all experienced AI chatbots getting confused by our long list of instructions. At first, if you start with something simple like ``generate an image of a water bottle'', it can give you a surprisingly accurate image. Then if you want to tweak something, like the size and color of the water tumbler, or if you want to add some specific graphical pattern to be printed on the bottle, the conversation just gets longer and longer. If you changed the color five times, added the graphical pattern, and then want to go back to change the color again, it becomes difficult to track at which step you changed to what color. It's also hard to reference a certain ``version'' of this long list of colors. At some point, your list of instructions gets long enough that the chatbot just gets confused and forgets what previously generated color you are referencing.

If you have some experience using chatbots, you will know the best way to start is providing as much information as possible in the first prompt, and being concise, direct, and commanding in your language. You will also know that models don't actually ``remember'' your conversations, so every new instruction you send out from the text box is just appended to all of the previous conversations, and this entire chunk of ``chat logs'' becomes the input for generating the next new response. For people with a bit of knowledge about LLMs, models all have a limit of ``context window'', the point at which they cannot process any more ``tokens'' and just ``forget'' the earlier conversations.

These are the affordances, properties, capacities, and limitations of the technology that should be reflected in the design of the interface to help the user better use it. However, when you first log into ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, none of this information is indicated to you. The fact that you can chat with a computer appears magical, but in fact, it makes it more difficult to use the technology. Overly emphasizing ``frictionless'' experiences in interface design only makes it a worse interface, because it deprives the user of the opportunity to learn about the technology.

At a deeper level, learning about how a technology works is crucial for people to start trusting it, and without trust, there is no adoption. This might seem contradictory because the emergence of the very idea of technology is that it's incomprehensible to a normal person without specific knowledge or education. (\cite{10.1162/1526381043320741}) Indeed, we don't need to know about logic gates and digital arithmetics to trust a computer, but through the desktop user interface we still learn a little bit about multiprocessing and memory management. We learned that computers can run things in the background and the files and programs you are not using still consume storage of your computer. Similarly for AI, the user doesn't have to know about all the messy matrix multiplications inside a Generative Pre-trained Transformer, but things like context window are still useful for users to ``have an idea'' of how it works and it also bears significant usability implications.

Trust is not just built by understanding but also by knowing that the technology is reliable, consistent and predictable. We trust a tool when we know that the same operation is always going to yield the same outcome. LLM is inherently stochastic, it's built to make predictions based on statistics. There is no guarantee that the same prompt will give the same outcome every time. However, the interface of chatbot presents itself as deterministic, sometimes even authoritative. We don't need to change the nature of AI. As a probabilistic tool it is already powerful enough to feel almost deterministic, but hiding the nature of AI behind authoritative language isn't going to build any trust. The illusion of AI having human-level conversational capabilities and trustworthiness is not only inaccurate but also detrimental for usability.

\subsubsection{Context}
With the uprising of OpenDevin, the terms ``agent'' and ``agentic workflow'' have suddenly become the hottest concepts in AI. What they are is not that much different from chatbots; in fact, it is the exact same type of LLM that powers both of them. These ``agents'' actually are a software wrapper around LLMs that allows them to directly operate your computer. Imagine you ask your favorite chatbot ``how do I add animated text to a video I took at my son's birthday?'', the chatbot will probably respond with a step-by-step guide for a certain video-editing software. If you have that software in your computer, you can follow the guide to complete the task; if you don't, you either tell the chatbot what software you actually have or you ask it how to install one.

With agents, the AI now takes a different approach. After you ask the question, it first directs the software wrapper to find out what video editing software you have on your computer, because the agent software can directly manipulate your computer. It then uses that information to generate the step-by-step guide for that specific software, and asks the software wrapper again to execute that plan to finish the task on your computer. It might be a little bit difficult to imagine this kind of workflow for every software task you do on your computer, but in the world of computer programming and software development, because there are already existing toolchains for the agent wrapper to operate a computer, ``agentic workflows'' have become a serious phenomenon.

What these agents have that the chatbots don't is access to a little bit more context. It is able to query the environment and state of your computer, and also directly operate it. With a normal chatbot, the user has to provide information like what video editing software you have. In fact, context is so important that Amelia Wattenberger said the majority of your prompt is context. If you ask a chatbot to write an email, you have to tell it who you are writing to, why you need this email, and how you want to sound in the email. The part of the prompt where you are actually specifying an action is just ``write an email that …'', everything else is basically context.

The problem with a chatbot is that providing every single real-world context in plain language is tedious. In the real world, every fact has structured relationships; in a chatbot environment, we are forced to outline them in detail in order for the AI to have a picture of what is going on. Agents are a little better in having access to your computer, but outside of the context of programming-related tasks, they still don't understand much of the ``context'', so you are left with your language and language alone.

Describing context with language is tedious enough, but what's even worse is when you want to switch between contexts. Traditional desktop interfaces use the idea of ``windows'' to manage context. A software is a window; all the tools it provides, the files you are working on, and the history of operations are all within the window and are shared knowledge for different components of the software. Windows also define clear boundaries between contexts. Photoshop is for editing photos, so you can only load image files into it and it only provides image-editing related tools. Similarly, Word is for text editing, so you don't perform complicated photo editing inside Word. You edit a photo inside Photoshop and then drag-and-drop it to Word. Windowing systems not only manage the boundaries of contexts but also provide passthrough between them and make switching easy. In a conversational chatbot interface, the idea of managing context is nonexistent. Again, all you have is trying to type an even longer prompt to provide contextual details. 

Humans are able to manage contexts natively because we understand the structure and relationships between facts (in fact humans might have invented the structure and relationships between facts). Computer algorithms, however, can still only understand a small fraction of our world. Making computers mimicking this kind of human behavior is simply a pie in the sky (for now).

\subsubsection{Language is abstract (the world is not)}
Context is not the only thing that is tedious to describe in language; in fact, most real things are very tedious to describe with language. Just try describing every detail in an image with words. Luckily, if what you want to do is to paint an image, people have already solved the problem by Graphical User Interface (GUI). Instead of telling your computer every detail of your image, you can just grab a virtual brush and paint on a virtual canvas displayed on your screen. What's even better is that your virtual painting is now also directly manipulable; just like a real canvas, what you see is what you get, and the changes you made are immediately perceptible. 

If you were to ask a chatbot to paint an image for you, you not only have to describe it in laborious details but also every new iterations or changes you want, you have to ask again, wait for the new outcome, and see if you want further improvements. This is almost the same way people would do it in the early days of computing, when the only available interface is command-line, and you type predefined commands for every task you want to perform. The history of human-computer interface is a downward slope of the level of abstraction, even though computers are made better with the idea of it. (\cite{10.1162/1526381043320741}) Compared to GUIs, conversational interfaces actually require a higher level of abstraction and is not a progression but a devolution.

Moreover, multimodal LLMs actually can understand other forms of information like images and sounds that are closer to our sensory experiences and less abstract, but the interface is still built around language only. Natural language is what LLMs are cut out to do, but the interface should be able to adapt to ``tangible actions'' and add more modes of information transmission. Instead of insisting on presenting an interface that indicates ``you can just talk to me for every kind of task as if I were a human'', we should consider the intrinsic properties of these AI models.

\subsubsection{Efficiency}
The argument of context and abstraction can also be summed up as efficiency. Our cognitive capabilities are shaped so that different types of information are most efficiently processed by different types of perception. \cite{efficiency} uses the idea of data compression to describe it. An emoji is able to convey ``face with tears of joy'' along with all the contextual, emotional information around that phrase instantly, but reading cannot achieve the same speed or even the same ``fidelity'' of that emoji. Additionally, there is an upper limit of our processing speed for natural language. We can only speak, listen, read, and write at a bounded speed, (\cite{reading-speed}) while processing native sensory experiences feels virtually instant. Similarly to the ideas behind the argument of context, we already have means to augment language with other types of information to increase the efficiency of communicating to a computer. Conversation is efficient for human-to-human interaction because we are able to employ all the sensory perceptions required by the fidelity of a conversation simultaneously with little effort. LLMs, however, can only understand the linguistic component.

Another aspect of efficiency is resource management. Anthropomorphic conversational interfaces make it feel like the AI is doing work effortlessly. But in reality, every prompt you submit consumes a quite significant amount of energy, water, and capital. Right now it feels like most AI tools are free, but I'd expect them to be less cheap very soon as resources are drained further into AI. Once we have to budget our AI usage, conversational interfaces are going to make it very difficult to gauge how computing-intensive a task is just by saying it. The cognitive load of processing a piece of information in our brain can be very different from the computational load of an LLM, because humans are able to employ different types of perception for different information, whereas LLMs still rely heavily on language. As resource extraction by AI development soars, reflecting the tangible cost of computation is crucial. Presenting AI as ``effortlessly human'' will be a very dangerous path.

\subsubsection{Moral status}
The deeper, more profound implications of anthropomorphizing a natural language processing algorithm is the induced moral status and relevant ethical considerations. As discussed in Sec. \ref{moralstatus}, along the way of developing a technology purely motivated by replicating human cognitive capability, inevitably at some point when the technology is ``close enough'' to a human being, we can no longer treat it just as a machine anymore. Once the technology develops certain level of ``consciousness'' and becomes a ``sentient robot'', it cannot be seen merely as means but ends and we need to consider its well being. Philosophers has provided several different examples of the thresholdd of inducing moral status, such as ``having sophisticated conception of oneself as persisting through time; having agency and the ability to pursue long-term plans; being able to communicate and respond to normative reasons...'' (\cite{bostromdeeputopia}) Through the conversational interface and the style of language used, the AI chatbots we are using today are not shy at all about aspiring to achieve these conditions. You could even argue that some of them are already met. But, are we ready to start considering the moral status of an algorithm?

This challenges the very fundamental idea of pursuing ``Artificial Intelligence'', whether we actually want to attempt at replicating human intelligence or not. In my opinion, what we call AI now is just another evolution in the long stream of innovations in machine learning algorithms. Its ultimate goal should just be an even higher level of automation in computational tasks, not the pursuit of giving machines agency or intelligence. In fact, I'd argue the automation of computational tasks is the only area where AI can be deployed efficiently and generate positive productivity growth. Science-fiction-like anthropomorphism is great for generating public momentum and interest, but ultimately the interface design should stay true to what the technology is actually good for, and refrain itself from pushing AI to inappropriate realms. 

At the usability front, the idea of moral status echoes some of the examples in Celine Nguyen's article (\cite{moral}):
\begin{displayquote}
If an LLM offers us incorrect information, or makes something up because the correct information is unavailable, it is lying to us; if it quotes a source that doesn't exist, it is making things up; if the LLM continues to offer incorrect information, even when we confront it, it's doubling down. The problem, of course, is that it's a little incoherent to accuse an LLM of lying. It's not a person. People can be expected to exhibit integrity. People have, in most situations, an ethical obligation to tell the truth. If they don't, you can't trust them.
\end{displayquote}
Fundamentally, LLMs are just linguistic pattern matching and predicting algorithms, and just like every other technology we created, there is always a margin of error. But because the anthropomorphic conversational interface presents itself as human-like, we start referring the errors as ``lying'', ``making things up'', or ``doubling down.'' These are concepts that inescapably trigger negative emotional responses, in turn sabotaging user experiences and productivity. Natural language is what LLMs are built to do, but not conversations. Conversation implies human behavior, and we start to attribute to it human moral status and values. Then, when it fails to perform, we hold it to human integrity standards. In this scenario, conversational interfaces are simply just unnecessarily annoying, frustrating, and ``manipulative''. We've all heard of stories of people getting into long quarrels with a chatbot and ending up being angry at the chatbot.

\subsubsection{Cognitive surrender}
\cite{surrender} describe the cognitive system of certain ways of using AI as cognitive surrender, borrowing Kahneman's framework of think fast and slow. (\cite{fastandslow}) In Kahneman's original theory, we have two different cognitive systems for different kinds of tasks. Thinking fast means the system of intuition (System 1), where the need for cognition is low and fast decision making is performed based on experiential justifications; thinking slow is deliberation (System 2), where need for cognition is high and slow analysis is done through rational justifications. Cognitive surrender acts a third system external to our brain that we simply transfer our entire agency to. 
\begin{displayquote}
Unlike cognitive offloading, which is typically strategic and task-specific (e.g., using GPS to navigate), cognitive surrender entails a deeper transfer of agency. Whereas cognitive offloading is a strategic delegation of deliberation, using a tool to aid one's own reasoning, cognitive surrender is an uncritical abdication of reasoning itself. It reflects not merely the use of external assistance, but a relinquishing of cognitive control: the user accepts the AI's response without critical evaluation, substituting it for their own reasoning. … In cases of cognitive surrender, the user does not just follow System 3: they stop deliberative thinking altogether.
\end{displayquote}
Using AI in itself is not a disaster; in fact, in many cases delegating certain tasks to a prediction algorithm that is able to process vast data and knowledge in ways humans can't is beneficial. The problem is that, as the researchers observed, cognitive surrender is characterized by ``passive trust and uncritical evaluation of external information.'' By giving up their agency and criticality when engaging with an external tool, the decision-making process essentially becomes driven by the tool. This is problematic because AI lacks situational judgement and reasoning capabilities grounded in human experiences. An LLM can only make decisions based on our input, whether it's the training data or prompt. But real-world decision-making requires a much higher level of contextual awareness that involves senses which are non-transferable to any computing systems right now, like emotion, affect, or value systems. AI does not have true human experience; everything it has is simulated through data. Can we really trust it in situations where real humans will be impacted by its decisions? Furthermore, our current institutions do not incorporate any systems that impose moral standards or accountability on a natural language processing algorithm. What if something goes terribly wrong? Isn't it the user who gave up their agency that ultimately has to be held accountable? 

What I am trying to argue here is that AI is a tool that we should engage deliberately, intentionally and critically. At some point, when we figure out the good use cases for AI, we should be able to trust it as a tool, but not as a fundamental cognitive system that takes away our decision-making agency.

It has, however, become very difficult for users to insist on their own agency when using a chatbot. From the same research, the authors noted ``(Its) fluent, confident outputs are treated as epistemically authoritative, lowering the threshold for scrutiny and attenuating the metacognitive signals that would ordinarily route a response to deliberation.'' Because conversational interfaces confuse the boundary between a statistical pattern-matching model and a true cognitive system, users are more inclined to yield their agency to the algorithm. Once again, conversation is a multi-mode information transmission mechanism that employs several human-exclusive perceptions and senses. By mimicking a profoundly human communication medium, the design of a chatbot attempts to take over our internal cognitive systems.

\subsection{Design Principles}
The main point I want to make is that, anthropomorphism in the design of human-AI interface paints an illusional perception of what AI actually is and what it is good for. Conversational interface overhypes the capabilities of AI and deceives users from its limitations. As a user interface, it has several usability issues and is pushing the application of LLM to inappropriate fields without any opportunity for examination.

I also want to be clear that I am not critiquing the technology of AI. The prevailing paradigm of AI is certainly problematic at the societal level, but that doesn't change the fact that LLMs are technically very impressive. What I am strongly criticizing here is the interface design and thus the narrative around AI. Every technology has its good and bad; it's all about discerning the good uses and the bad ones. This is traditionally where interface design has a big role to play, and my criticism is that the anthropomorphic conversational interface has failed dreadfully in that regard.

So how do we reverse the wrong illusion of AI caused by anthropomorphism? I want to propose a new approach towards designing interfaces of AI that is based on the idea of making AI machine-like. That is, designing interfaces that are directly linked to how the system functions. The interface should hint to the user directly what the limitations of the system are and make perceivable the internal changes originated from user inputs. In machines, the knobs, dials, and buttons are often physically connected to the mechanical shafts, pulleys, and gears. This is the most directly manipulable system that provides instant feedback and also informs the user how it works. Such an interface demands human attention and in no way takes agency away from the user. It is the extreme opposite of an anthropomorphic conversational interface that pretends to have human-level intelligence. A machine-like interface makes it clear that it is a tool that the user engages with deliberately and its output should be examined and evaluated by humans.

Here I sum up a couple of principles for designing machine-like interfaces for human-AI interactions.
\begin{description}
    \item[Frictional] Clunkier is better. Instead of being hyper-fixated on creating seamless user experiences, a machine-like interface requests as much human input as possible. It presents a wide range of controls that need to be configured in order for it to work well. It doesn't correct user input unannounced; instead, it raises warnings or simply fails. It leaves critical decision-making entirely to the human operator. It makes the interaction deliberate and very intentional.

    What is the friction we can add to LLM interfaces? Maybe it's an additional step between submitting the prompt and the actual model kicking in that asks you to review if your prompt provided enough information and context. Or, maybe the idea of a messaging text box should be abandoned, and instead users are required to submit the prompt through a structured form that ensures details are provided.

    The success of LLMs is rooted in scaling. They perform proportionally (or even exponentially) better as you scale up the number of parameters inside a model. However, because of the sheer computation required by even a moderately sized model, LLMs also become significantly slower and more resource-hungry as they get bigger. Since size is such a defining trade-off of the technology, maybe we should also give users more granular control over the size of a model, or even let them configure the internal dimensions for different components of the model. The size of the model should also be made very clear to the user instead of being hidden behind marketing jargons like ``ultra'', ``max'', or ``plus''.
    \item[Attention-seeking] A machine-like interface requires monitoring and constant examination from the user. The user has to be aware of the fact that something can go wrong and be prepared to jump in. The interface should not encourage the user to just ``forget about it'' and wait for the machine to complete everything perfectly; instead, it should clearly communicate its internal state and ``system load'' to the user. At critical junctures, the interface tries to involve the human and requests evaluation from the human user. 

    Current interfaces of LLMs sometimes provide an ``abort'' button for longer tasks, but they don't really encourage the user to jump in. Borrowing an analogy from music playback control, an interface can be made very simply by giving the user another ``rewind'' button, so as the latest output of the model is streamed to the user, they can hit pause, rewind to a certain point, and restart the process from there with potentially more instructions. This gives the user more incentive to constantly monitor the system because now they can actually catch and fix an error before the entire output is generated. Maybe add another dial for controlling speed too, so the user can decide when they want to closely monitor the output in critical parts of the task.

    Even simpler interventions, like showing how much of the context window is occupied in real time, can also increase the incentive for users to pay attention to the internal state of the model and potentially provide more opportunities for them to optimize their usage.
    \item[Informative] If something fails, you should be able to find the setting that caused the problem and fix it. The interface should show that the system is directly manipulable — you see a wheel spinning faster as you turn the dial. Through the feedback-optimize loop, the user gradually learns about the system and translates that knowledge to their unique methodology of operation. A machine-like interface is the complete opposite of a black box. It represents the nature and affordances of the system, clearly showing its capabilities and limitations. It should also make the trade-offs obvious and provide means for resource management.

    One opportunity to make the interface more informative is to highlight the probabilistic nature of LLMs. LLMs are inherently stochastic, but the anthropomorphic conversational interface always presents itself as deterministic and authoritative. Imagine at certain important junctures, the model does not just spit out the most likely prediction but gives the user a choice of the three most likely predictions. This is a simple way to give users more control and also highlight important internal properties of LLMs.
\end{description}

All of these ideas use the interface to push more agency back to the user. They emphasize the fact that AI is fundamentally a tool we use; it performs as optimized by the human operator. And the human user is ultimately accountable for ``using it the wrong way.'' The machine-like interface does not attempt to replicate human emotion or pretend to understand human experiences. It's designed to stay true to what it is, so we can, too.
